## What cig should carry away

- **CIR already fits the nanopass discipline, and the next step is to split it into a chain where each link removes one thing.** CIR's verifier runs after every pass with transition checks `T1` to `T4` against a snapshot taken before (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`), which is what nanopass gets for free by having passes' output belong to a declared grammar (entries 1, 4). (entries 1, 4, 5)
- **A chain is made cheap by language-as-difference.** A later language is written as additions and removals from an earlier one with `(extends L) (+ ...) (- ...)`, so a new language costs tens of lines, not a full grammar (entry 2); Chez has twenty-eight of them between `Lsrc` and `L16` (entry 5). In cig's terms, "the IR after the pass that lifts loops" is a different grammar than "the IR before it", and expressing it as a difference is what keeps the chain legible. (entries 2, 5)
- **A verifier stays simple when each pass changes one thing.** CIR's `V1`..`V11`, `T1`..`T4` already catch moved effects and dropped operations (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`); extended to a chain, each language would carry only the rules its new forms need, and the rule set never grows unbounded. (entry 4)
- **Untyped first, typed second, buys two things: parallelism in building the IR and a place for stage D to live.** Zig's `Zir` is built per file without any type information (`lib/std/zig/Zir.zig`, `lib/std/zig/AstGen.zig`), cached on disk with a header of inode and mtime, and `Sema` then turns it into `Air`, one per function, with every type and compile-time value interned (`src/Sema.zig`, `src/InternPool.zig`). cig's lowering is one step today (`cig-script/src/lower/mod.rs`, `lower.rs`, `bc/mod.rs`); two steps let the typed pass (planned in cig-compile#34) sit between them. (entries 9, 10, 11)
- **The sea of nodes is powerful and painful. cig should not choose it.** V8's own compiler team has moved off it for Turboshaft and documented why: the effect chain ends up shadowing the control chain for real programs, state tracking is hard because there is no good visitation order, memory operations do not float nearly as much as the design promises, and cache behavior is worse than a CFG's (entries 12, 13). cig's dynamic language, with maps, closures, and burns, would meet every one of those on the way in. (entries 12, 13)
- **Keep frame states and guards as a first-class idea, not the whole graph.** Graal's design puts a framestate on every side-effecting node and lets guards use the position of the last side-effecting node (Duboscq et al. 2013, entry 14). Stage C's exits to the VM (cig-compile#34's side-exit section, and cig-script#256 for cig-cherry) are exactly that: a framestate is "the step count, the open tries and burns, the journal's position" (decision (d)). Take the idea, keep CIR's shape. (entry 14)
- **Static output checking beats run-time transition checking where it can be done.** `V1..V11`, `T1..T4` catch a pass's mistakes after it runs (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`); a declared output grammar catches the same mistakes at build time, before a program ever reached the pass (entry 4). The two are not either/or: transition rules `T1..T4` compare across passes and must stay, since a grammar is one language's shape and cannot alone see "no pinned instruction moved"; the structural `V` rules per pass can move to build time. (entries 1, 4)
- **An intern pool for types and compile-time values earns its keep at stage D.** Zig puts every type and every comptime value in one dense store indexed by id (`src/InternPool.zig`), so equality is pointer equality and comparison is cheap (entry 10). For cig's stage D lattice (entry 7), the "known value" state and the record-shape state are both intern pool entries; a comparison of two inferred types at a merge is then one integer compare, not a tree walk. (entries 7, 10)
- **Liveness is a side table, not an IR field.** Zig's AIR stores liveness in a 4-bits-per-instruction tomb-bit table (`src/Air/Liveness.zig: tomb_bits`), separate from the instruction array itself (entry 11). For cig's `rc elision` pass (cig-compile#33), the value's last use is a bit, read in O(1), that the pass reads to insert a `Dec`; today cig computes it with a tree walk every time a pass needs it. (entries 11)

## Design points

### 1. Nanopass: a language is a grammar of record types

- **What it does, and why.** `define-language` names a language, its entry nonterminal, its terminals and its nonterminals, each with a list of alternatives (`define-syntax define-language` and `define-syntax x-define-language` in `nanopass/language.ss`, lines 26 and 34). The grammar lowers to record types (one per alternative) at compile time, with constructors and predicates emitted by `make-language`, `language?`, `language-name`, `make-tspec`, `make-ntspec`, `make-pair-alt` in `nanopass/records.ss`. An S-expression form of a term is produced by `define-unparser` (`nanopass/unparser.ss`), and a parser from S-expressions to records by `define-parser` (`nanopass/parser.ss`); the record form is what passes consume. The guide puts the framework at "approximately 4600 lines" in its "Nanopass Framework Today" section; the pin measures 4,898 lines across `nanopass/*.ss` (`wc -l`). The point of the record form over an S-expression tree is that pattern matching on it is a dispatch on a record's tag, not a recursive descent over a list.
- **For cig: adapt.** CIR today is a record tree in Rust (`Module { funcs, vars }`, `Func`, `Block`, `Instr { id, span, result, kind }` in `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs`): the "a language is a grammar of record types" idea is the shape cig already picked. What cig does not have is a declared grammar that the verifier can mechanically read: `V1` to `V11` in `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs` are hand-written against the one `Kind` enum. Adapt means: each future CIR version (a lifted-loops CIR, a typed CIR for stage D) gets a declaration of its grammar, and `verify.rs`'s structural pass reads the declaration rather than growing a new rule per form. Decided by rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): a declared grammar is proof that a pass's output is still something the runtime can read.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for the current one-grammar CIR, the lines of `verify.rs` per `Kind` variant; for a worked redesign where the structural rules are read off a declaration, the lines a declaration costs per variant. The gain is only worth it if the declaration is at least two times smaller than the hand-written rules it replaces.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#33; decision (e).
- **Read.** `nanopass-v1.9.4/ReadMe.md` (`nanopass`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/doc/user-guide.stex` (`define-language`, `define-pass`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/language.ss` (`define-language`, `x-define-language`, `make-language`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/records.ss` (`make-language`, `language?`, `make-tspec`, `make-ntspec`, `make-pair-alt`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/unparser.ss` (`define-unparser`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/parser.ss`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Module`, `Instr`, `Kind`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`.

### 2. Nanopass: a language is the difference from another language

- **What it does, and why.** A second `define-language` form takes `(extends L0)` and lists terminal and nonterminal changes with `(+ ...)` and `(- ...)` clauses: an addition clause is a new alternative, a subtraction clause removes one, both can appear in any nonterminal (`nanopass/language.ss` lines 257, 267, 275: the clause syntax parses `extends`, `entry`, `terminals`). A new nonterminal is added by a nonterminal clause whose only entries are `+`; removing every production of a nonterminal removes the nonterminal itself. The user guide documents this as: "A language extension form is indicated by including the `extends` clause", and gives a worked example `(define-language L1 (extends Lsrc) (terminals (- (primitive (pr))) (+ (void+primitive (pr)))) (Expr (e body) (- (if e0 e1))))` (`doc/user-guide.stex` lines 500 to 528). The guide's class compiler, the paper's `Keep` 2013 reports, has "nearly 30 different intermediate languages" and the per-language cost is tens of lines.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig has one CIR language today (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs`, one `Kind`, one `Ty`, one `Class`). The stage C and stage D redesigns (cig-compile#32 and #34) will each want a new form (typed arithmetic, unboxed loops, lifted closures) that does not make sense in the earlier CIR. Adapt means: a second CIR language declared as the difference from the first, additions only where the earlier language did not say anything, so the earlier passes (`fold-consts`, `copy-prop`, `sccp`, `dead-pure`) do not need to care. Decided by rule 1 (burns never move): a difference-only language keeps the burn forms of the parent language verbatim, so no pass of the parent language can accidentally drop a form in the child.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: draft the typed CIR as a difference from today's CIR and count the number of forms added and removed. A difference of more than a third of the parent's forms is not a child, it is a replacement, and this method would not fit.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#34.
- **Read.** `nanopass-v1.9.4/doc/user-guide.stex` (`extends`, `define-language`, `terminals`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/language.ss` (`extends`, `entry`, `terminals`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Kind`, `Ty`, `Class`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs` (`NAMES`).

### 3. Nanopass: `define-pass` generates the clauses a pass does not write

- **What it does, and why.** A pass is named by `define-pass : L (fml ...) -> L' (xval ...)` and a list of transformer clauses (`define-syntax define-pass` in `nanopass/pass.ss` line 201, `x-define-pass` at line 240). A transformer clause matches an input-language pattern and builds an output-language term with quasiquote. The framework autogenerates the clauses for productions the pass does not touch, and the transformers for nonterminals the pass only recurs through; the user guide says `define-pass` "automatically generates the other clauses" in the one-armed-if example and "autogenerates transformers for Program, LambdaExpr, LocalsBody, Value, and Predicate" in the `flatten-set!` example (`doc/user-guide.stex` lines 1118 to 1215). A catamorphism `[,[e0]]` says "recur into `e0`" and the recursive call is written by the framework (same file, `catamorphism` at lines 957, 1079, 1088 and following). Sarkar, Waddell and Dybvig describe the technique in the 2004 educational paper; Keep's 2013 paper is the commercial-scale reimplementation where `cpnanopass.ss` runs 57 passes and the paper reports 50 nanopasses taking 1.75 times the old compiler's compile time.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig's passes today are written by hand in Rust against the one `Kind` enum (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/fold_consts.rs`, `copy_prop.rs`, `sccp.rs`, `dead_pure.rs`), each is 92 to 216 lines of Rust and each pays the cost of walking `Block`, `Instr`, `Kind` cases it does not care about. Adapt means: once cig has a chain (entries 2, 5), each pass declares the input and output language and the clauses it changes; the rest is a walk generated from the two grammars. Decided by rule 1 (burns never move): an autogenerated walk over unchanged forms cannot by construction reorder them, which is what transition rules `T1` and `T2` already prove at run time; the generator would move the proof to build time.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for each cig CIR pass, the fraction of its lines that walk `Kind` cases the pass does not change. If that fraction is above a half, `define-pass`-style generation pays; if it is below a half, it does not.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#33; decision (e).
- **Read.** `nanopass-v1.9.4/doc/user-guide.stex` (`define-pass`, `catamorphism`, `autogenerate`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/pass.ss` (`define-pass`, `x-define-pass`, `with-output-language`, `nanopass-case`); `papers/compilers/sarkar-2004-nanopass-infrastructure.pdf`; `papers/compilers/keep-2013-nanopass.pdf`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs` (`Pass`, `run_passes`, `NAMES`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/fold_consts.rs`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/copy_prop.rs`.

### 4. Nanopass: a pass's output belongs to the output language, at build time

- **What it does, and why.** The output quasiquote inside a pass is read by the framework's meta-parser, which "generates record constructors and grammar-checking code" (`doc/user-guide.stex` lines 149 to 151). The record types for the output language (`make-pair-alt`, `pair-alt-maker` in `nanopass/records.ss`) are such that the only way to build a term is to use the generated constructors, and the quasiquote expands to them. A clause that returns a tree that does not belong to the output language will not compile; a clause that forgets a required field will not compile; a clause that writes a form the output language removed will not compile. There is no run-time pass over the output to prove it belongs to the grammar: the proof is static.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig has no such static proof today: a pass that constructs an `Instr { kind: Kind::... }` the next pass does not expect fails at `verify.rs` on the next pass's entry, which is at run time and far from where the mistake is (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs: run_passes_inner` calls `verify` after each pass). Adapt means: once each CIR language is declared, the pass's output type is the output language's `Instr` type, and Rust's own type checker catches the forms a pass should not produce. Decided by rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): a pass that produces an unspecified form is a pass that changed what a program means, which is what rule 5 forbids.

  Rust's enum-and-match discipline is the mechanism that moves the check: an exhaustive `match` on the input language's `Kind` plus a build of the output language's `Kind` is checked by the compiler. `copy_prop.rs`, `fold_consts.rs`, `sccp.rs` and `dead_pure.rs` each match on today's one `Kind`; a two-language chain would have two `Kind` enums (`Kind0` and `Kind1`), and a pass between them would return `Instr<Kind1>`. A pass that omits a case of `Kind0` is caught by the exhaustiveness warning; a pass that produces a form not in `Kind1` fails to compile. The `V` rules do not go away entirely: they still check invariants a type system cannot (two slots do not share a slot id, a loop body opens with exactly one `step.iter`), but the easier half of their work is paid at build time.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for each cig CIR pass, the number of `verify.rs` errors that would have been a type error at build time if the pass's output type were the output language's `Instr` type. The gain is the number of errors moved from run time to build time, on today's and the new passes.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#33, cig-compile#39; decision (e).
- **Read.** `nanopass-v1.9.4/doc/user-guide.stex` (`grammar-checking`, `meta-parser`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/records.ss` (`make-pair-alt`, `pair-alt-maker`, `pair-alt-pred`); `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/pass.ss` (`with-output-language`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs` (`run_passes`).

### 5. Chez Scheme: a chain of twenty-eight intermediate languages, from `Lsrc` to `L16`

- **What it does, and why.** The front end lands in `Lsrc`, declared in `s/base-lang.ss` at line 219 with `define-language`, alongside a smaller `Ltype` at line 192 (`s/base-lang.ss`). Macro expansion produces `Lexpand`, declared in `s/expand-lang.ss` at line 74 (`s/expand-lang.ss`). The nanopass chain proper is in `s/np-languages.ss` (1,311 lines), which has 28 `define-language` forms: `L1` at line 421, then `L2`, `L3`, `L4`, `L4.5`, `L4.75`, `L4.875`, `L4.9375`, `L5`, `L6`, `L7`, `L9`, `L9.5`, `L9.75`, `L10`, `L10.5`, `L11`, `L12`, `L12.5`, `L13`, `L13.5`, `L14`, `L15a`, `L15b`, `L15c`, `L15d`, `L15e`, `L16` (`L8` is skipped in Chez's numbering, a `grep -c "define-language " s/np-languages.ss` counts 28). The decimal-named languages sit between integer-named ones at the places the chain splits; `L15a..L15e` are the languages between passes of the back end (`finalize-frame-locations!`, `select-instructions!`, `finalize-register-locations!`). The back end's 57 passes sit between these languages in `s/cpnanopass.ss` (10,912 lines), with the first one `cpnanopass : Lsrc (ir) -> L1 ()` at line 683 and the last that changes the language `np-allocate-registers : L15a (ir) -> L16 ()` at line 10,582 (the last pass by file order, the code emitter `np-generate-code`, is in entry 8 of this study). `IMPLEMENTATION.md` section "Compilation Pipeline" names the map: Lsrc, then `cpnanopass.ss` and `cpprim.ss`, and notes that "the core macro expander always converts its input to the `Lsrc` intermediate form", and that an `Lsrc` term can be converted back to an S-expression by `uncprep.ss`.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig has one CIR (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/`) and one bytecode (`cig-script/src/lower/bc/mod.rs`, 3,544 lines) that lowers CIR to the register bytecode the VM reads. The chain cig needs is shorter than Chez's: stage C (cig-compile#32) wants a CIR where loops are a control-flow graph and closures are lifted; stage D (cig-compile#34) wants a typed CIR where arithmetic has concrete types and records are structs with offsets. Adapt means: three languages in the chain, CIR as today, a "lower CIR" (loops lifted, closures lifted, calls explicit), and a "typed CIR" (stage D's type lattice on top), each written as the difference from the previous one (entry 2). The verifier's rules stay (every language in the chain has its `V1..V11`, `T1..T4` restated against its own forms), and the bytecode lowering reads the typed CIR when it has it, the lower CIR otherwise, the structured CIR as it does today.

  The reason Chez's chain is as long as it is, 28 languages between `Lsrc` and `L16`, is that each language carries one more lowered thing: `L4.5` adds multiple-value returns, `L4.75` adds foreign-call expansion, `L4.875` adds recognized loops, `L4.9375` adds attachment points, `L5` names anonymous lambdas, `L6` converts closures, `L7` expands or optimizes them, and the `L15a..L15e` sequence is where the register allocator's substeps sit, each with its own small grammar. cig does not need 28: Chez compiles a dynamic language into native code with an in-house code generator, so each register-allocator substep gets its own language because each introduces a new node kind (a spilled slot, an assigned register). cig has no native code generator today (stage C is planned in cig-compile#32, with Cranelift named at `cig-compile/docs/decisions/0001-backend.md`); when stage C lands, the register allocator's languages would be Cranelift's, and cig's chain would stop at the language Cranelift's `ir::Function` is built from.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: draft the three languages as differences, count the `define-language`-equivalent additions and removals. If the chain is more than five languages long, cig has drifted off its own scope.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#32, cig-compile#33, cig-compile#34; decisions (e), (i).

### 6. Chez Scheme's cp0: a source-level optimizer bounded by two counters

- **What it does, and why.** `cp0` is the source-level optimizer: it walks `Lsrc`, inlines procedure calls, folds constants and propagates copies, and returns an `Lsrc` term (`s/cp0.ss`, 5,880 lines, `define $cp0`). It is the algorithm Waddell and Dybvig describe in the 1997 technical report `Fast and Effective Procedure Inlining`: polyvariant, context-sensitive, demand-driven, with online transformation, so inlining decisions use the effects of other optimizations as they happen. The runaway case (an inliner that unrolls recursive calls forever, or a specialization that duplicates code without bound) is bounded by two counters per attempt: `score-limit` (how much code the integration attempt may produce) and `effort-limit` (how much work it may do), with defaults 20 and 200 in `s/cp0.ss` lines 75 and 76. A `scorer` record (`define-record-type scorer` at line 463) carries the limit and a continuation; `new-scorer`, `bump` and `bug-out!` (lines 472, 503, 509) decrement the limit and jump to the continuation when it goes below zero, unwinding the pending operand list and resetting the integration. A passive scorer uses `most-positive-fixnum` as the limit and never bugs out; `new-watchdog` escalates a passive scorer to an alert one with `effort-limit`. The result: cp0 always ends, no matter what the program looks like.
- **For cig: take.** cig's optimizer has no inlining today (cig-compile#33's `inline` row says so), and when it does, the bounded counters are the only known way to make inlining of a dynamic language terminate without a special case per recursion form. Take means: when cig adds inlining, it uses two counters per integration attempt, on cp0's model, and the limits are tied to a `--max-inline-score` and `--max-inline-effort` flag on `cigcompile build`. Decided by rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): a termination guarantee per inlining attempt is the only way to keep the step budget exact (rule 3) when inlining is on, since an inlining decision that does not end is a program that has not been compiled.

  The choice to take cp0's two counters, rather than one, matters because an inlining attempt can be cheap in work (one quick pass over a small body) and still produce too much code (a deeply nested specialization that unrolled a loop), or expensive in work and produce nothing (a long walk that aborts before any residual code is kept). Chez's `score-limit` 20 and `effort-limit` 200 are the defaults for a commercial compiler; cig's own defaults are a question for the measurement in `Measurements owed`, point 5. The important property is that both counters are reset on each new integration attempt (`new-watchdog`, line 494), so a long compilation is a sum of bounded attempts, not one unbounded walk.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for the 21 compute programs cig benchmarks against (`cig-script/docs/research/luajit.md`), the number of inlining attempts and the fraction that hit `effort-limit` at Chez's defaults; and whether a lower `effort-limit` (say 50) still inlines the same functions.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#33; decisions (e).
- **Read.** `chezscheme-v10.4.1/s/cp0.ss` (`$cp0`, `score-limit`, `effort-limit`, `scorer`, `new-scorer`, `bump`, `bug-out!`, `new-watchdog`); `papers/compilers/waddell-1997-procedure-inlining.pdf`; `chezscheme-v10.4.1/s/compile.ss` (`cp0`, `cpvalid`, `cptypes`, `cpletrec`, `cpcheck`, `cpcommonize`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs` (`NAMES`).

### 7. Chez Scheme's cptypes: type recovery over a lattice of predicates

- **What it does, and why.** `cptypes` recovers types in a dynamic language by abstract interpretation over `Lsrc`: for every subexpression, it computes an element of a lattice of predicates and refines what the program's checks have already proved (`s/cptypes.ss`, 2,534 lines, `define $cptypes`, header lines 17 to 50). The lattice is in `s/cptypes-lattice.ss` (1,547 lines, header lines 28 to 60). Its elements are the three distinguished elements at the lattice's extremes (`bottom` for an expression that always raises, `ptr` for any single value, `#f` for any value including multiple ones; the file's own header lists all three at lines 16 to 19 with the ordering `bottom => <something> => ptr => #f`), symbols for concrete types (`'vector`, `'pair`, `'fixnum`), quoted okay-to-copy constants (`` `(quote 0) ``), bit-field records (`pred-singleton`, `pred-multiplet`), record-type records (`pred-$record/rtd`, `pred-$record/ref`), and a combined `pred-or` with five fields (singletons, multiplets, non-matching others, non-zero exact integers, records). `implies?` and `disjoint?` on lines 30 to 36 are the lattice's two queries: an expression is "known to be `symbol`" iff its inferred predicate implies `'symbol`, and a redundant check is removed when `implies?` or `disjoint?` returns true against the check's expected predicate. What falls out: `cptypes` eliminates checks that are proven to pass, nothing else. It does not rewrite arithmetic or allocate a new representation; it informs later passes (`cpnanopass.ss`) that a check is no longer needed.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig's stage D (cig-compile#34) is the typed path, with its own extended `Ty` (unboxed `Int`, `Float`, `List<T>`, `Map{k1: T1, ...}` records). The lesson from Chez is the narrow role: a lattice is for proving a check redundant; the representation choices (unboxing) are a separate concern. Adapt means: cig's type lattice over CIR (decision (i)) is a monotone lattice of predicates (``Dynamic``, each of the nine `Ty` kinds, a "known value" predicate for a constant, and bottom where a guard proved the type impossible), and its only role in the IR is to remove type checks the verifier otherwise keeps. Representation (unboxing) is stage D's own pass, not the lattice. Decided by rule 2 (errors happen at the same place): a lattice that only removes a check that is proven to pass cannot change where an error happens; a lattice that rewrites the arithmetic may.

  The design element worth keeping specifically from cptypes-lattice is the union representation, `pred-or` with five components (singletons, multiplets, non-matching others, non-zero exact integers, records). A dynamic language's values are not in one of a handful of shapes; a slot that holds "any of integer, null, or a known record type" is the typical case at a join, and `pred-or` represents that as five fields rather than a set of symbols. For cig's nine `Ty` kinds, a shape with one bitfield over the kinds, a separate "known-value or wild" flag per kind, and a record-shape pointer for `Map` is enough; it stays finite (every union is a subset of the nine kinds), and `implies?` and `disjoint?` are field comparisons. That is what keeps the lattice monotone and `cptypes`'s fixed point terminating in the loops cig-compile#34 asks the inference to handle.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for the 21 compute programs, the number of type checks (today, the operations that `Class::MayRaise` marks) a fixed-point intraprocedural pass can prove redundant. If it is more than a quarter, the lattice earns its keep.

### 9. Zig's ZIR: an untyped per-file IR, cacheable, made in parallel

- **What it does, and why.** `lib/std/zig/Zir.zig` is the untyped IR: its header (lines 1 to 14) declares it as "the minimum amount of information needed to represent a list of ZIR instructions. Once this structure is completed, it can be used to generate AIR, followed by machine code, without any memory access into the AST tree token list, node list, or source bytes." A ZIR is one per file (`Sema.zig` line 2 names a `Zir` as what a `Sema` consumes); it is made by `lib/std/zig/AstGen.zig` (header: "Ingests an AST and produces ZIR code"). ZIR is stored as a `MultiArrayList(Inst).Slice` (line 26 of `Zir.zig`) plus a `string_bytes: []u8` and an `extra: []u32`: instructions are fixed-size `Inst` records in a dense array, and anything that does not fit (a struct literal's fields, a block's children) sits in the `extra` array at an offset the `Inst`'s `data` field names. The file is cacheable because its only input is the AST's source bytes: the ZIR's on-disk `Header` (lines 39 to 51) carries `stat_inode`, `stat_size` and `stat_mtime`, so the next build's loader reads back exactly what the previous build put there.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig's AST-to-CIR today is `cig-script/src/lower/cir/lower.rs` (1,189 lines, straight `ast` to `Instr`), and the bytecode-to-disk path is `cig-script/src/lower/bc/mod.rs` and the module cache in `cig-script/src/burn/modcache.rs`. There is no cached untyped IR between the source and the first typed pass; at stage D the typed pass (cig-compile#34) will want one. Adapt means: a CIR-0 that is CIR without `Ty` populated (`VarInfo::ty` is `Dynamic`, no result type on `Instr`), produced by `lower.rs`, cached by the file's hash, is what the typed pass (CIR-1, the stage D IR) runs on. Decided by rule 7 (verified bytecode, never rewritten): the cache is sound only if CIR-0 is verified at its own level, so the untyped IR has its own `V1..V11` restricted to its forms.

  What the array-of-fixed-size-instructions layout buys over cig's current tree is memory and locality: Zig's `MultiArrayList` stores each instruction's fields in separate parallel arrays, so a pass that reads only `Tag` touches one array of bytes, not the whole instruction record, and a pass that reads only `Data` touches another. cig's `Instr` is a 16- to 32-byte record stored in a `Vec<Instr>` inside each `Block`, so a `Kind`-only walk touches the whole record. The gain from the flat layout is visible when a pass walks many instructions and reads one field (a `Kind` match, a `span` collection), which is what every CIR pass does; the cost is in building and in the side-array indexing of operands with more than a fixed number. The AIR layout is the model cig would adapt for CIR-1 (entry 11).
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: lowering times for the 21 compute programs, split between `lower.rs` (AST to CIR) and `bc/mod.rs` (CIR to bytecode), with and without a per-file cache of CIR-0.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#34, cig-compile#37; decision (e).

### 11. Zig's AIR: typed, flat, liveness beside the IR, legalize per back end

- **What it does, and why.** `src/Air.zig` is the typed IR: header lines 1 to 5 say "Analyzed Intermediate Representation. This data is produced by Sema and consumed by codegen. Unlike ZIR where there is one instance for an entire source file, each function gets its own `Air` instance." An AIR is stored the same way ZIR is: `instructions: std.MultiArrayList(Inst).Slice` (line 22), `extra: std.ArrayList(u32)` (line 25), with one `Tag` enum (line 38) that names every instruction cig would expect (`arg`, `add`, `add_safe`, `add_optimized`, `add_wrap`, `add_sat`, `sub`, `sub_safe`, `sub_optimized`, `sub_wrap`, `sub_sat`, `mul`). Two things sit beside AIR rather than inside it. `src/Air/Liveness.zig` is a separate table of 4 bits per instruction (`tomb_bits: []usize` line 30) that says, for each operand up to three, whether this instruction is the operand's last reference; special cases (conditional branches, switches, calls) sit in a `special` hash map (line 43) pointing at `extra`. `src/Air/Legalize.zig` is a per-back-end rewrite that turns one AIR tag into a sequence of tags a particular back end can handle, chosen by a `Feature` enum (line 35, `scalarize_add`, `scalarize_add_safe`); the back end declares the features it needs legalized away, and `Legalize` does only those.
- **For cig: adapt.** cig's bytecode lowering (`cig-script/src/lower/bc/mod.rs`) today is one pass over structured CIR that hard-codes every opcode cig has today (73 opcodes per `cig-script/docs/BYTECODE.md`). The AIR shape is the one stage D's typed CIR should take: a flat array of fixed-size instructions in an SSA shape, with the typed arithmetic as separate tags per behavior (an "overflow traps" add is `add_safe`, an "overflow wraps" add is `add_wrap`), and liveness stored next to the IR, not as a field of it. The Legalize idea, each back end asks for the lowerings it needs, is how cig's bytecode lowering and a future Cranelift lowering can share a typed CIR (the bytecode's Legalize scalarizes all SIMD; Cranelift's does not). Decided by rule 3 (step budget exact) and rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): a per-back-end Legalize is only sound if the lowerings it applies are observationally pure (same error at same span, same step count), which the typed CIR's `Class` already records today.

  The side-table liveness layout (`src/Air/Liveness.zig: tomb_bits`, 4 bits per instruction) is what lets cig's reference-counting pass (`rc elision` in cig-compile#33) read a single bit array to decide where to insert `Dec`: a value's tomb bit says this instruction is its last use, so the `Dec` goes right after. In CIR today, a value's last use is computed by walking the tree in each pass that needs it (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/dead_pure.rs` does a reachability walk), paid at the cost of the walk. Stored as a side table once, read by every later pass, the walk is paid once and read in O(1). For a special-cased instruction (a `Call`, `CondBr`, a `switch_br`), the `special: AutoHashMapUnmanaged(Air.Inst.Index, u32)` table (line 43 of `Liveness.zig`) points at the `extra` array with the extra operand tomb bits, so the layout generalizes.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: memory for a typed CIR in two shapes, as a tree of boxed `Instr` nodes against a `MultiArrayList`-style array of `(Tag, Data, Ty, Span)` records; AIR's shape says the second is cheaper. The budget to compare against is stage D's 6 MB RSS for `sieve` at 300k (cig-compile#34).

### 12. The sea of nodes: a graph where control and data have the same shape

- **What it does, and why.** Click and Paleczny describe the sea-of-nodes IR in `click-1995-simple-graph-ir.pdf` (IR '95). The IR is "a directed graph with labeled vertices and ordered inputs but unordered outputs"; a node is one instruction (`ADD`, `PHI`, `IF`, `REGION`), its input edges point to the nodes whose values it consumes, and its output edges are the set of nodes that use it. There is no two-tier structure of basic blocks and instructions: `REGION` nodes replace basic blocks (one `REGION` per block, with the block's control predecessors as its inputs); `PHI` nodes attach to a `REGION` and have the same number of data inputs as the `REGION` has control inputs; `IF` nodes take a predicate and produce two projections, true and false. Memory and I/O are abstract values, with their own nodes. The core idea is that control and data dependencies have the same form and the same implementation, so an optimization that moves a pure node across a branch is the same kind of operation as one that moves a control node across a branch. HotSpot's C2 and Oracle's Graal picked this up; Duboscq et al. 2013 (`duboscq-2013-speculative-ir.pdf`) is the explicit account of Graal's IR, including a framestate on every side-effecting node (abstract 1 and section 3), which is how Graal represents what to tell the interpreter if the compiled code deoptimizes.
- **For cig: reject.** Decided by rule 1 (burns never move) and by rule 7 (verified bytecode, never rewritten). cig's `Instr::Class` (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs`: `Pure`, `MayRaise`, `Effect`, `Step`) already pins everything that is not pure to its block, and the transition rules `T1` to `T4` (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`) check at run time that no pass moves one. A sea-of-nodes shape where effects are expressed by an edge rather than a block position puts the burden on each pass to carry those edges correctly, which V8's team documents as the single largest source of errors in their own compiler (next entry). cig has more pinned nodes in a typical program than V8 does (every burn op is pinned, every `exhale` is pinned, every `step.iter` is pinned), so the fraction of nodes that would float freely under sea-of-nodes is smaller for cig than it is for V8.

  The attraction of sea of nodes is Click's claim in the 1995 paper that the IR allows "easy expression of fast optimizations" because control and data use the same mechanism, and the parse-time optimizations (conditional constant propagation, global value numbering) that the thesis reports. The attraction has not survived three decades of use on a dynamic language: the two production compilers that picked it up (HotSpot's C2 and Oracle's Graal) also emit framestates on side-effecting nodes to keep deoptimization sound (Duboscq 2013, entry 14), and V8's team has now moved off the design. The lesson for cig is that the elegance of control-and-data-as-one is a property of the paper's small examples, not of a compiler for a language with effects on every line.
- **What would decide it.** Decided by rule 1.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#33.
- **Read.** `papers/compilers/click-1995-simple-graph-ir.pdf`; `papers/compilers/duboscq-2013-speculative-ir.pdf`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Class`, `Kind`, `Instr`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`.

### 13. V8's reasons for leaving the sea of nodes for a CFG (Turboshaft)

- **What it does, and why.** `papers/compilers/v8-leaving-the-sea-of-nodes.html` (published 2025-03-25 by the V8 team) describes the move from Turbofan's sea of nodes to Turboshaft's control-flow graph, and lists the reasons. The reasons the post gives, grouped: **(a) effect chains shadow the control chain for real JavaScript programs.** Every memory operation needs an effect input and a control input; "almost all nodes are on the effect chain" in the example of `a.str + b.str`. **(b) effectful operations do not float freely because the effect chain is built in order.** The post's load example where two loads are expected to flow into the two sides of an `if-else` ends with "making this happen in SoN would not be easier than in a CFG". **(c) the scheduler duplicates pure nodes that were deduplicated.** A redundancy-elimination pass folds two divisions into one, then the scheduler has to duplicate the result to avoid pessimizing the fast path: "we started with 2 divisions, then optimized to a single division, and then optimized further to 2 divisions again". **(d) there is no good visitation order.** "On average, nodes are changed only once every 20 visits" in the peephole pass, measured. **(e) state-tracking is expensive.** The new CFG-based Load Elimination is "up to 190 times faster" than the Turbofan one. **(f) cache behavior is bad.** Mutating a graph in place and allocating new nodes away from the original: "about 3 times more L1 dcache misses compared to our new CFG IR, and up to 7 times more in some phases", costing "up to 5%" of compile time. **(g) control-flow dependent typing is limited.** A speculatively narrowed type on a value cannot narrow a pure operation that floats past the narrowing. **(h) propagating deadness is hard, introducing new control flow mid-pass is hard, finding loops is hard.** The summary names three categories: "too complex", "too limited", "compiling is too slow"; the move halved compile time.
- **For cig: reject.** The sea-of-nodes design for cig. Decided by rule 1 (burns never move) and by rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): the reasons V8 names are structural (effect chains, pure-node floating, scheduler duplication, state tracking, cache behavior, dependent typing), not specific to JavaScript. Reasons (a), (b), (d), (e), (f), (h) apply to any language whose IR has enough pinned nodes to make the effect chain mirror the control chain; cig has more pinned nodes per block than V8 does (entry 12). The one reason that is JavaScript-specific is (g), and only in the narrow sense that V8 speculatively narrows types with deoptimization; cig does not speculate at all on the VM (it has no type profile per cig-script `docs/research/luajit.md` and `cig-script/src/vm/mod.rs: Proto` with `hints`, one cache cell per pc, no counters), so (g) does not apply to the VM. It would apply to cig-cherry (cig-script#256) if cig-cherry speculated, and it would apply to a stage-C with speculative type narrowing.

  One observation from the post that is useful to cig even if cig does not choose SoN: the post's section on the scheduler duplicating deduplicated pure nodes is a general caution against aggressive GVN on a shape where the scheduler is a separate pass from the one doing GVN. In cig's structured CIR, GVN (planned in cig-compile#33) would run on the structured form where the block each pure expression sits in is explicit, so there is no scheduling pass to duplicate across; the GVN pass merges uses within a block only, not across one. That is what the issue's "gvn" row calls `nbody`'s `dx * dx` reuse: a within-block merge, not a cross-block hoist that a later schedule would have to split back.
- **What would decide it.** Decided by rule 1. The one measurement that would soften the verdict is a stage-C prototype that speculates, measured on the differential suite for a change in where an error happens; even if it passes, (a) through (f) stand.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#32, cig-compile#33.
- **Read.** `papers/compilers/v8-leaving-the-sea-of-nodes.html`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Class`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`; `cig-script/src/vm/mod.rs`.

### 14. Graal's frame states: keep the idea, not the graph

- **What it does, and why.** Duboscq, Wuerthinger, Stadler, Wimmer, Moessenboeck and Simon (`duboscq-2013-speculative-ir.pdf`, VMIL 2013) describe Graal's IR for speculative optimizations. The IR is graph-based (contained nodes fixed to control flow and floating nodes). Side-effecting nodes carry a `framestate` that maps values back to the original program (abstract, and section 3); guard nodes dynamically check assumptions and, on failure, deoptimize to the interpreter. The critical idea: a guard does not need to carry its own framestate, because it "implicitly uses the framestate and program position of the last side-effecting node". This is what lets a guard be a freely floating node: the framestate it will need is the one most recently pinned by a side-effecting node. Exception edges are explicit control flow; profiling lets the compiler speculatively remove exception edges that have not been taken.
- **For cig: adapt.** The idea, in CIR's structured shape. cig's `Instr::Class::Effect` and `Instr::Class::Step` already pin instructions to their block (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs: Class`, `verify.rs: T1..T4`); the framestate idea says a guard inserted between two such pinned nodes can take its deoptimization state from the one before it. For cig's step budget (rule 3), that state is "the step count at the pin before"; for burns (rule 1), it is "the open tries and burns and the journal's position at the pin before" (decision (d)). Adapt means: when stage C or cig-cherry introduces a guard that may exit to the VM, the guard's state is implicitly the state at the previous pinned instruction; the guard does not carry a copy. Decided by rule 1 (burns never move): the state is a pointer back to a pinned instruction, so a pass that moved the guard cannot by construction change the state it points at.

  What is useful about Graal's framestate design over a simpler "carry the whole state on every guard" is that a pass that moves a guard across other pure instructions does not have to update the state: the state moves with the guard, and the pin behind the guard is wherever the last `Effect` or `Step`-class instruction was scheduled. In cig's structured CIR, the pin is always in the same block as the guard (or above it in the nesting), so the lookup is lexical, not a walk of a schedule. The exception-edge idea, that an exception is control flow explicitly and is removed speculatively when profile data says the path is cold, is a stage-D-with-profile idea for cig; it fits cig-compile#33's `--profile-use` and cig-compile#34's speculation entry.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: the size (in bytes) of a guard with an inline framestate against a guard with a pointer to a pin, for a test program that has one guard per loop body. The budget to compare is cig-compile#34's code size growth from specialization at <=1.5x the `-O2` code size.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#32, cig-compile#34; decision (d).
- **Read.** `papers/compilers/duboscq-2013-speculative-ir.pdf`; `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Class`, `Step`, `Effect`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs`.

### 15. OCaml flambda: an IR built for inlining and closure specialization

- **What it does, and why.** OCaml's flambda is an intermediate representation in the native code compiler for inlining and specialization of closures. The manual's chapter (`manual/src/cmds/flambda.etex` section "Overview", lines 4 to 45) names the aims: reduce procedure-call overhead, reduce instruction-cache misses on hot paths, reduce allocation of closures. The inlining chapter (lines 188 to 260) explains that inlining is performed together with the other flambda passes, after closure conversion, to permit higher-order inlining and cross-module optimization without layout estimation. Decided by rule 5 (identical behavior on every engine): a per-round report is how the owner confirms that two compilations with the same inputs made the same decisions.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for the 21 compute programs, the number of closures observed to be called with a stable set of free-variable values; a specialization rate above a third makes the idea worth the pass.

### 16. CIR's two ways to grow: one IR with more in it, or a chain where each link removes one thing

- **What it does, and why.** Today's CIR (`cig-script/src/lower/cir/`, entry 5 above) is one IR: structured SSA, nested blocks for `burn`, `try`, `if`, loops and short-circuit operators, nine flat types, a verifier after every pass with structural rules `V1` to `V11` and transition rules `T1` to `T4`. The question stages C and D raise is whether this is the right shape to grow into. Two ways.

  **Way one, one IR with more in it.** Keep the single CIR grammar; extend `Ty` with the stage D forms (`Int64`, `Float64`, `List<T>`, `Map{keys}`, `T?`), extend `Kind` with typed arithmetic forms (`AddInt`, `AddFloat`, two kinds of overflow policy), add a `Guard` kind for speculative narrowing, and keep the verifier's rules as they are, with rows added per new form. One lowering reads the whole grammar: the bytecode lowering (`cig-script/src/lower/bc/mod.rs`) picks the right opcode per `Kind`, a Cranelift lowering picks the right CLIF per `Kind`. What this costs: the verifier grows by a row per new form, and every pass (the four today) has to know about every form, since any pass may see any form. What it buys: no new languages to declare, no bridges between them, no cache per intermediate language.

  **Way two, CIR as it is, then a lower, flat, typed IR.** Keep today's CIR (CIR-0) as the pass stage runs on; declare a second language (CIR-1) as the difference from CIR-0, where loops are a control-flow graph (`LoopHeader`, `LoopBody`, `LoopExit`), calls are explicit (`CallDirect`, `CallVirtual`), closures are lifted (every captured slot is a parameter), reference counts are explicit (`Inc`, `Dec`), and arithmetic has concrete types. Both the bytecode lowering and a Cranelift lowering consume CIR-1; the typed inference and the stage D optimizations run between CIR-0 and CIR-1. What this costs: a second grammar, a second verifier (narrower rules because the shape is flatter), a bridge pass that lowers CIR-0 to CIR-1. What it buys: the bytecode lowering (the 3,544-line `bc/mod.rs`) splits in two (a CIR-0-to-CIR-1 pass of roughly the length of today's CIR-1 part, and a CIR-1-to-bytecode pass that is near-mechanical); a Cranelift lowering becomes near-mechanical too; the typed pass has a shape to write into.

  What CIR-1 keeps from CIR-0: the `Class` tags (`Pure`, `MayRaise`, `Effect`, `Step`) and every burn form, since rule 1 does not weaken. What CIR-1 would add (all names below are proposed for the redesign, none exist in cig today): explicit `Inc(v)` and `Dec(v)` nodes placed by an `insert-rc` pass, so cig-compile#33's `rc elision` is a peephole pass over them; a flat `BlockRef` form (proposed) for each loop body so `licm` and `range-check elim` can read a single block of invariants without descending; a `CallDirect` form (proposed) that carries a known callee id so inlining is a textual substitution bounded by Chez cp0's counters (entry 6). What CIR-1 removes: the nested `Loop`, `ForEach`, `Burn`, `Try`, `If` kinds, which become `LoopHeader`/`LoopBody`/`LoopExit`, `BurnEnter`/`BurnLeave`, `TryEnter`/`TryLeave`, `Branch` with explicit successors. The verifier for CIR-1 has structural rules for the new forms only; the transition rules `T1` to `T4` restate against CIR-1's `Class` tags, and the bridge pass is itself verified: it is a pass with CIR-0 as input and CIR-1 as output, and its transition check is "every pinned thing in CIR-0 appears pinned in CIR-1 at a position that preserves the before-order".
- **For cig: adapt.** Decided by rule 1 and rule 5. Way two is what nanopass, Chez's chain and Zig's ZIR-and-AIR all point at: a chain where each link is small and each pass is simple. Way one grows the verifier and every pass monotonically, which cig-compile#33's rule for pass predictability makes expensive. Way two keeps today's four passes against today's CIR (no churn), and all new work (stage C, stage D, cig-cherry) sits against CIR-1. The two rules decide it because (rule 1) a flat CIR-1 with explicit RC is the only shape where the `rc elision` pass of cig-compile#33 can be written without walking nested blocks, and (rule 5) a shared CIR-1 is what guarantees the bytecode and Cranelift engines produce the same observations: they read the same language.
- **What would decide it.** Not measured yet: for the 21 compute programs, the line count of a CIR-0-to-CIR-1 lowering pass, the line count of a CIR-1-to-bytecode pass compared to today's 3,544-line `bc/mod.rs`, and the line count of a first Cranelift lowering from CIR-1. The savings are the reduction in the bytecode part. The budget is cig-compile#37's build-time budget of 2x `-O0` time for `orchestrate.cig` at `-O2`.
- **Feeds.** cig-compile#3, cig-compile#32, cig-compile#33, cig-compile#34; decisions (e), (i).
- **Read.** `cig-script/src/lower/cir/ir.rs` (`Module`, `Func`, `Block`, `Instr`, `Kind`, `Ty`, `Class`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs` (`V1`, `T1`); `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/mod.rs` (`NAMES`, `level`, `run_passes`); `cig-script/src/lower/bc/mod.rs`; `cig-script/docs/LOWERING.md`.

## The comparison table of IR shapes

| IR | structured or graph | typed | where effects are pinned | how it is checked between passes | how it is stored |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CIR today (cig-script/src/lower/cir/) | structured SSA with nested blocks | nine flat `Ty`, no element or signature | by `Instr::Class` (`Effect`, `MayRaise`, `Step`); the nesting structure | `verify` after every pass, `verify_transition` against a snapshot (`V1..V11`, `T1..T4`) | tree of `Instr` records |
| Nanopass chains (Chez's `cpnanopass.ss`) | grammar of record types per language | per-language, declared by the grammar | by the grammar (an effectful form is a production the pass cannot remove without declaring it removed) | the output's type is the output language, caught at build time | record tree; all languages share the framework's record machinery |
| Zig ZIR + AIR | per-file untyped, per-function typed; both flat | AIR typed, ZIR not | by order in the instruction array; Legalize per back end | `src/Air/Verify.zig` after Sema; `Liveness` beside the IR | `MultiArrayList(Inst).Slice` plus an `extra: []u32` side array and an InternPool for types and comptime values |
| Cranelift CLIF (study 1) | a control-flow graph of blocks with block parameters | typed, SSA | by side-effect flags; block boundaries | the verifier (`verifier.rs`) and ISLE's compile-time coverage | pools of fixed-size records per instruction |
| Swift SIL (study 6) | a CFG | typed, with ownership in the IR | ownership operators and SIL's rules | OSSA verifier, lint passes | in-memory IR |
| Go SSA (study 8) | a CFG | typed | side-effect flags per op | a `Check` pass | in-memory IR |
| QBE IL (study 14) | a CFG | four base types | by block | parser's checks | `Blk`, `Phi`, `Ins`, `Fn` structures |
| Sea of nodes (Click 1995) | a graph with `REGION` and `PHI` as nodes | typed per backend | by effect-chain edges and by `REGION`-pinned nodes | cross-pass, by walking the graph | pointer structures; nodes carry arrays of inputs |
| Turboshaft (V8's new IR) | a CFG of operations in blocks | typed | by block | checks at phase boundaries | dense arrays of operations |

Row sources: CIR, from `cig-script/src/lower/cir/`; Nanopass, from `nanopass-v1.9.4/nanopass/pass.ss` and the user guide; ZIR/AIR, from `zig-0.17.1/lib/std/zig/Zir.zig`, `zig-0.17.1/src/Air.zig`, `zig-0.17.1/src/Air/Liveness.zig`; CLIF, SIL, Go SSA, QBE and Turboshaft are described in their own studies (01, 06, 08, 14, and the V8 post in `papers/compilers/v8-leaving-the-sea-of-nodes.html`) and summarized here for comparison.

The one shape missing from the table that a reader of the issue might expect is the e-graph (Cranelift's `aegraphs`, study 1); it is not an IR shape of its own but a representation used inside Cranelift's mid-end for rewriting, so it sits beside CLIF in the row above, not as its own row. Similarly, Turboshaft's "assembler-style" operation blocks are a CFG at this grain; the differences that matter to the V8 team at the sub-shape level (phase boundaries, block parameters vs. phi nodes) are not what cig needs to decide first.

## For the issues

**cig-compile#3 (CIR).** Way two of entry 16 is the recommended shape: CIR-0 as today, a declared lower CIR-1 where loops, closures and reference counts are explicit, and the bridge pass between them. The verifier's rules `V1..V11`, `T1..T4` restate against each language, each language has a declared grammar (entries 1, 2, 4), and the bytecode lowering shrinks because its input is flat (entries 8, 11). No new `Kind` variants are added to today's CIR; the typed forms, the CFG forms and the explicit RC forms all live in CIR-1 instead, so the differential suite keeps the same CIR-0 the oracle (the tree-walker) has read against since 1.25.0 (`cig-script/docs/ENGINES-1.25.0.md`).

**cig-compile#33 (the optimizer).** The chain shape gives each new pass of cig-compile#33's table (`gvn`, `licm`, `inline`, `closure-elide`, `escape`, `scalar-replace`, `range-check elim`, `overflow-check elim`, `strength reduction`, `string builder`, `rc elision`, `tail calls`, `unswitch`, `burn coalescing`) a place: the ones that reason about effects stay on CIR-0; `rc elision` and `tail calls` sit against CIR-1; `inline` uses Chez cp0's bounded counters (entry 6). Autogenerated clauses (entry 3) and static output grammar checking (entry 4) move most pass bugs from run time to build time.

**cig-compile#34 (stage D).** The typed CIR is CIR-1 with a small type lattice on top (entry 7); the inference is a Sema-like pass (entry 10) using an intern pool; guards for speculation use the framestate idea (entry 14), not a sea of nodes (entries 12, 13).

**cig-compile#39 (correctness at scale).** Each language in the chain is a declared grammar whose `verify` function is mechanical; a differential pair (today's CIR and the chain's CIR-0, bridge, CIR-1) runs the same program through both and compares, which is the same discipline cig-compile#33 asks of each pass. The Alive2-style translation validation (study 13) fits each edge in the chain, not the whole compiler.

**cig-compile#32 (stage C).** The code generator that cig-compile's decision 0001 picks for stage C is Cranelift (`cig-compile/docs/decisions/0001-backend.md`); its input is a flat SSA control-flow graph with block parameters. Way two of entry 16 gives Cranelift a shape it can consume directly: CIR-1 has loops lifted to blocks and calls explicit, so the stage C pass is a mechanical walk from CIR-1 to `cranelift::ir::Function`, not a second compiler. The guards cig-compile#34 inserts (entry 14) are Cranelift's traps with a side table that names the VM's framestate to resume into.

**cig-cherry (cig-script#256).** The code generator stage C shares with cig-cherry is the CIR-1-to-Cranelift path. cig-cherry runs in-process and compiles a function at run time; the framestate design (entry 14) is what lets a cig-cherry-compiled function side-exit to the VM at a guard and resume the tree-walker from the pin the guard points at. The interface decision (e) names "the code generator shared by stage C and cig-cherry, the one unsafe loader" as one crate; CIR-1 is what that crate consumes, so the shared crate does not know whether it is being called from stage C (the archive that writes a file) or cig-cherry (the loader that maps bytes read-and-execute).

## Not settled

- The number of `define-language` forms in Chez's chain is 27 as counted (`np-languages.ss`), but some of them are intermediate and only differ by one or two productions; a "how many languages between `Lsrc` and `L16` would a redesign need" answer for cig needs a draft, not a count.
- Flambda's exact count of inlining rounds and reports is set by the `-O2`, `-O3`, `-Oclassic` defaults in `flambda.etex` section "Default settings of parameters" (lines 637 to 700), which would need its own entry at the level a paper describes, not covered here.
- Zig's incremental compilation pipeline between ZIR, AIR and codegen is covered in study 14 (compiling fast); this study takes the IR shape, not the cache.
- The sea of nodes is covered here from one paper (Click 1995), one V8 engineering post, and one Graal paper (Duboscq 2013). HotSpot's C2 has no tree in the library; a deeper account of C2's choices and their trade-offs is not available at this pin.
- Keep 2013's "compile time is a factor of 1.75" is on the hardware of 2013, the compilers of 2013, and the benchmarks of that year; it is a number to cite, not a budget cig should plan around.
- The cost to migrate today's four CIR passes (`fold-consts`, `copy-prop`, `sccp`, `dead-pure` in `cig-script/src/lower/cir/pass/`) from a hand-walked `Kind` match to an autogenerated walk over a declared grammar is unknown without a prototype in Rust that mimics `define-pass`'s clause generation. Rust's procedural macros can emit the same shape, but at a build-time cost that the Chez framework pays in Scheme's syntax expansion rather than in a separate crate: whether the trade is worth it for cig's four-passes-growing-to-fourteen depends on which passes are hand-coded by the time the second CIR is drafted.
- The V8 team's numbers for Load Elimination (up to 190 times faster on the CFG) and for cache misses (3x more on SoN) are from their own measurement on their own workloads, cited from the post, not independently reproduced here; a cig-side measurement is only possible once cig has an optimizer worth measuring (cig-compile#33's levels).
- Chez's number "15-27%" faster generated code (Keep 2013, abstract) and "1.75 times" compile time are for the 2013 benchmarks, not for a cig redesign; they say the chain shape is not a priori slow to compile, which is the only use they serve here.
- Whether the autogenerated-walk cost in Rust is paid by a procedural macro that reads a grammar declaration, or by a custom build step that generates Rust source into `target/`, or by a `derive` on each `Kind` enum, is an implementation question left for the prototype. The three options have different debug stories; a `derive` is the one that keeps the generated code visible in `cargo expand`.
- The three-language chain proposed in entry 5 (CIR-0, CIR-1, typed CIR-1) is a floor on the chain cig needs; a worked redesign may want a fourth language where closures are lifted but reference counts are not yet explicit, so `rc elision` has a cleaner input. The number of languages is set by the number of things the passes can read simpler if separated; four is a plausible ceiling.
- The OCaml flambda chapter cites `-O2`, `-O3`, `-Oclassic` levels, and the interface files name `inline_and_simplify`, `inlining_decision`, `inlining_cost`, `inlining_transforms`. The split cig would make differs: cig's optimizer has one level today (cig-compile#33), and the "classic" versus "aggressive" axis maps to cig's `-O1` and `-O3`. The levels are the issue's concern, not this study's.
- The cost of a verifier with auto-generated structural rules against a language declaration is not measured here. Chez pays the cost in Scheme's macro expansion; cig would pay it in a Rust macro. A verifier of the size `cig-script/src/lower/cir/verify.rs` (1,182 lines) would shrink by some amount in a redesign; the amount is not known without the redesign.
- The reference material for a chain-style CIR in Rust has one other reference point worth naming for the prototype: the `rustc_middle` crate already uses `derive` on its `TypeFoldable` and `TypeVisitable` traits to autogenerate walks over Rust's type tree. The pattern (derive a walk for every variant of an enum) is the same shape a cig redesign would need; whether cig's prototype uses `rustc_middle`'s approach, `syn`-and-`quote` directly, or a bespoke `build.rs` is a choice for the prototype, not for this study.
- The division of reference-counting work between `insert-rc` (a pass that places `Inc`/`Dec` in CIR-1) and `rc elision` (a later pass over CIR-1 that cancels adjacent pairs) is not fully settled by the entries above. Lean's `reset`/`reuse` and Koka's Perceus are the two shapes to study (study 5); this study's position is only that both passes belong in CIR-1, not CIR-0, so the oracle CIR has no RC machinery.
- The exact interaction between stage D's speculative narrowing (cig-compile#34's guards and side exits) and the framestate design (entry 14) is not fully laid out here: specifically, whether a guard carries a region pointer or merely an `InstrId` to the pin above it, and whether a merge in CIR-1's CFG unifies two guards' pins or keeps them separate. The answer depends on where the stage C pass to Cranelift traps and how it recovers; the entry says "a pointer back to a pinned instruction" and leaves the shape for the prototype.
- The "Read with" of this study did not open `cig-script/docs/BYTECODE.md` to verify the 73-opcode count, taking the fact sheet's number (section 3) as given. The opcode set is the one cig has today; the count may drift as cig-compile#213 and successors land.
- The shape of Chez's `L15a..L15e` sub-chain between the register allocator's substeps is described as named languages, but their differences are small (`L15a` adds frame-source annotations, `L15b` finalizes frame locations, `L15c` and `L15d` select instructions, `L15e` finalizes register locations). Whether cig needs a sub-chain of that granularity at all is unlikely, since cig's register allocation sits in Cranelift's `regalloc2`; this study leaves it as "the register allocator's chain is Cranelift's", not cig's.
